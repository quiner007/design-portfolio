Beamdown.

An action/adventure boss rush where you beam down to dying planets, fight through their beasties, and purify each one in a boss fight. I owned combat, enemy, and level design — the decisions that determine whether a player can actually read a fight.

The game

A research trip that's really a boss rush.

Beamdown is a 3D top-down hack-and-slash boss rush that keeps a straight face about its own premise. You play E.R.O. 4 — the shiniest new research operative the Societas Philanthropia has ever built — beaming down to strange planets to "research" them.

In practice, research means carving through each world's beasties and decimating its guardian, then customizing your upgrades between fights until you're a dash-happy melee blur or a walking wildfire. The deeper you go, the more you start to wonder what the Societas Philanthropia is really after.

My role

My job was to make sure players understood a fight before it killed them.

I set the combat, enemy, and level design for the whole game — how a fight feels moment to moment, what each enemy is there to teach, and how a level tells you where to go without ever putting a marker on screen.

A boss rush lives or dies on legibility. You have to understand what a planet is teaching you before it tests you on it, read an attack in the half-second you have to react, and always know where the fight is. Almost every decision on this project came back to that.

The problem

A boss rush is unreadable by default.

In a boss rush the player meets an unfamiliar mechanic and a health bar at the same moment. If they can't tell what the boss is doing, why they just died, or where the fight even is, difficulty stops reading as a challenge and starts reading as unfair — and they quit.

So the real design problem was legibility. Teach a mechanic before the boss examines the player on it. Make every attack readable in the half-second there is to react. Never let anyone wonder where to go next. Get those right and a hard game feels fair; get them wrong and no amount of balancing saves it.

Goals

What Beamdown had to do.

  • Make combat feel powerfulSnappy, engaging fights, and augments that visibly change how you play — the player should feel the upgrade, not read about it.
  • Teach before you testGrunt areas introduce a planet's mechanic so the boss room can examine the player on something they've already met.
  • Raise difficulty, not the wallEvery enemy should make a fight harder, never impossible. Losing has to feel explainable.
  • Never lose the playerLighting and layout answer "where do I go" and "what matters here" without a waypoint.
Process

Designing a game you can read.

1 · Wrote the pillars as player-experience statements

Before anything got built I wrote three pillars, and I wrote every one of them from the player's side of the screen rather than the team's. Not "ship a combat system" — combat needs to be snappy, engaging, and fun. Not "build levels" — use lighting and other level design tropes to make sure the player knows where to go and what is important.

The enemy pillar opens with a question instead of a requirement: Boss design is #1. What is the experience of fighting each boss? That framing did a lot of work later — it meant every enemy argument started from what the fight feels like, not from stat blocks.

Three is deliberate. The point of writing pillars down is to have something to lose arguments to: when two good ideas competed, the pillars decided it rather than whoever talked longest.

2 · Built the core combat loop

The loop is simple and readable on purpose: beam down, fight the beasties, purify the planet in a boss fight, then level up your stats — and beam down again. Bosses are the star, so grunt encounters exist to teach a planet's mechanic before you're tested on it.

Augments feed that loop — each one should improve how the game plays and make the player feel stronger the next time down.

The diagram got redrawn until it fit in four boxes. That was the test: if a teammate couldn't read the loop at a glance, it wasn't finished — a diagram that needs explaining isn't doing its job.

3 · Used the level to do the teaching

Grunt areas are short in player time but dense on purpose. Lighting and layout carry the wayfinding, so the player is pulled toward what matters instead of being told. Then every boss room reflects the mechanic that planet has spent the last few minutes teaching — the fight is the exam, and the level was the lesson.

You can read the whole intent off this map. The player spawns alone at the far end, so the first thing that happens is movement. Trenches close off the wrong answers. Valves and a key-and-gate slow the critical path just enough to make the space feel deliberate. Logbooks sit slightly off it, so story is a reward for curiosity and never a tax on people who just want to fight. A mini-boss lands before the real one as a rehearsal.

Every planet got mapped on paper before anything got built in Unity — moving a gate on a printout costs nothing, and moving it in engine costs an afternoon of someone else's week.

4 · Protected the design all the way to ship

Design decisions only count if they survive production. I ran the board so the player-facing work — boss rooms first, then the levels teaching into them — kept its place in the schedule instead of being the thing that got cut when time ran short.

Boss areas were the number-one priority in the pillars, so they were the number-one priority in the plan. That's the whole reason the teaching-then-testing structure made it into the shipped game rather than staying a nice diagram.

A hard game only feels fair when the player can see why they died.

Outcome & reflection

What shipped, and what I learned.

Beamdown launched publicly on Steam with an 83% positive rating — a complete action/adventure boss rush, difficult on purpose, that players stuck with.

The lesson I kept is that legibility is a feature, and it's cheaper than balance. Almost everything that made the game feel fair was decided before a single value got tuned: what the level teaches, what the boss asks, and whether the player can see the difference. Tuning numbers can't rescue a fight the player can't read.

The other lesson was about volume. The pillars, the loop, and the maps exist because generating options is cheap and committing early is expensive — so I made a lot of them, then used the pillars to throw most of them away.
