The work

Research that development can actually use.

I led a student research team inside Riot's research program, coordinating deliverables across QA, social, and marketing so that what we found came back as one structured set of findings rather than three disconnected opinions.

I also went to E3 that summer, and the final piece I delivered was a competitive analysis — looking at what the rest of the market was doing and what that implied for League.

Research Team LeadCompetitive analysisCross-functional coordinationSynthesis
My role

My job was the part between having findings and them mattering.

Leading the team meant deciding what we were actually asking, splitting it across QA, social, and marketing so each function looked at the thing it could see best, and then pulling all of it back into something a developer could read and act on.

The coordination was the hard part. Three functions produce three different kinds of evidence, and if nobody reconciles them, the team hands over a pile rather than a point of view.

The problem

Player feedback is loud, plentiful, and mostly unusable.

A game the size of League never lacks opinions. What's scarce is the version of those opinions a development team can do something with — separated from noise, attributed to a real pattern rather than the loudest thread, and framed as a problem instead of a demanded solution.

That's the gap the research existed to close, and it's the same gap I've been working in ever since. Years later on Eldritch Confection I was doing a smaller version of exactly this: taking twenty-five survey responses and figuring out which of them described a real trend.

Goals

What the research had to do.

  • Ask one clear questionFindings are only comparable if everyone set out to learn the same thing.
  • Use each function's vantageQA, social, and marketing each see a different slice of the player — point them where they can see best.
  • Synthesise, don't collectHand over a point of view, not a folder.
  • Land it where it countsStructure the findings so they could feed back into development rather than stopping at a summary.

The job isn't gathering what players said. It's telling one loud opinion apart from a real trend.

Outcome & reflection

What I took from it.

The team delivered structured findings into League development and I closed the summer with the competitive analysis piece. It was 2018 and I was a student, so I'll be honest about the scale of it — but it's the first time I saw research treated as a discipline with a standard rather than as asking people what they thought.

The habit it gave me stuck: decide what you're trying to learn before you collect anything, and take the synthesis as seriously as the gathering. That's the same loop I ran weekly on Eldritch Confection, and it's the part of UX I keep coming back to.
