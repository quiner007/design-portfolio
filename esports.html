The work

Two jobs, one audience problem.

I started at Santa Barbara City College, where there wasn't a Rainbow Six Siege program yet — so I built one and coached it. That meant recruiting, running practice, and teaching a roster to read a round the same way.

Then three years at the University of Utah coordinating live esports broadcasts: regional tournaments, streamed to a public Twitch audience, with a crew of twenty to thirty people running production.

Coaching and broadcasting look like different disciplines. They aren't. Both come down to taking something fast and complicated and making it legible to someone who wasn't in the room when it was decided.

My role

I decided what the viewer sees, and when.

Coordinating a broadcast means owning the run of show and the people executing it — casters, observers, graphics, replay, and everyone keeping the stream alive between matches. My job was to keep that crew in sync while the game kept moving, because a live match doesn't wait for you to catch up.

The design half of it is choosing the frame. An observer pointed at the wrong player turns a decisive moment into something the audience only hears about afterwards. Getting that right, repeatedly, under time pressure, is a real-time interface problem.

The problem

A match is illegible from the outside.

Competitive games are designed to be read by the people playing them. Spectators get none of that context. They can't see cooldowns the way a player feels them, don't know what the plan was, and often don't know why a round just ended.

So the broadcast has to do the work the game doesn't: point the camera at the thing that matters before it matters, give the caster what they need to explain it, and keep a newcomer oriented without boring the people who already know the game. That's an audience with two very different levels of expertise watching the same stream.

Goals

What the broadcast had to do.

  • Never miss the momentBe pointed at the play before it happens, not cut to it afterwards.
  • Serve both audiencesReadable for someone who just showed up, without narrating the obvious to people who play at a high level.
  • Keep the show aliveDowntime between matches is part of the broadcast, not a gap in it.
  • Run it with the crew you haveTwenty to thirty people, mostly students, on real deadlines — the plan has to survive contact with a live event.

Live production has no undo. You either designed for the moment before it happened, or you missed it.

Outcome & reflection

What I took from it.

Three years of tournaments went out live, and the program at SBCC existed because I built it. Neither leaves a Figma file behind, which is exactly why they taught me something the design files didn't.

Live production is the most unforgiving version of experience design I've done. There's no iteration once the stream is up — you get one attempt, in front of an audience, and every choice about what to show is made in the second it's needed. It made me much faster at deciding what matters and cutting everything that doesn't.

It's also where I learned to design for expertise as a spectrum rather than a persona. The same broadcast has to hold someone who has never seen the game and someone who plays it competitively, and pretending they're the same viewer fails both.
